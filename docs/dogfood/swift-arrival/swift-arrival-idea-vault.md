# Swift Arrival — Outlandish Idea Vault

Status: unfiltered brainstorm, not a committed scope

Purpose: preserve funny premises, events, cargo, routes, vehicles and escalation chains

## North star

*Swift Arrival* is a first-person 1–6 player delivery roguelite where a group of underqualified employees physically operate a large walk-through truck, handle absurd cargo with floppy arms, survive a branching workday and may finish the run in a completely different vehicle.

The comedy should emerge from understandable systems colliding:

> The player knew what they wanted to do, the game understood them, and physics made the result catastrophically different.

## Challenge without frustration

### Core rules

- Make the **arms look unreliable while the intention remains reliable**.
- Challenge should come from incompatible goals, time pressure and teamwork—not from inputs being ignored.
- Telegraph danger before it becomes destructive.
- Escalate mistakes into new situations instead of immediate mission failure.
- Preserve partial success: damaged deliveries, late deliveries and missing packages still earn something.
- Let players choose greed. Extra contracts, illegal cargo and shortcuts should create most high difficulty.
- Give every severe problem at least one desperate recovery method.
- Use soft deadlines that reduce bonuses before using hard deadlines that end runs.
- Never destroy essential cargo through an off-screen random event.
- Keep quiet recovery beats between intense events.
- Make failure attributable: players should know which corner, loose strap or terrible decision caused it.
- Make blame funny and shared rather than singling out one player for punishment.
- Scale complexity, not just health and damage.

### Forgiving physical controls

- Generous hand target selection.
- Grab sockets on handles, straps, wheel and important cargo.
- Short input buffering when a hand barely misses.
- Stable two-hand grips.
- Heavy objects visibly strain arms before grip failure.
- Cap absurd collision impulses that would launch vital cargo out of the map.
- Automatically prevent small objects becoming permanently wedged under the truck.
- Recovery winch for inaccessible cargo.
- Emergency `Recall Package` service that returns an item heavily damaged and charges an insulting fee.
- Camera remains stable while the body and arms absorb most impacts.
- Quick recovery after a tumble.
- Arcade steering with readable momentum rather than realistic truck simulation.
- Reverse camera, mirrors and a passenger lookout all remain useful.
- Optional comfort settings for camera shake, head bob and tumble rotation.

### Fail-forward states

- Broken crate becomes loose contents.
- Cracked egg becomes an escaped dinosaur problem.
- Gas leak becomes a ventilation problem.
- Missed ferry becomes a dangerous night route.
- Police seizure becomes an impound-yard retrieval job.
- Wrecked truck becomes an emergency vehicle-transfer event.
- Stolen cargo becomes a pursuit.
- Dead battery becomes a push-start or roadside generator problem.
- Wrong delivery becomes a retrieval mission from an increasingly confused customer.
- Banknote spill becomes a roadside cash chase.
- Destroyed bridge opens a ferry or construction-site detour.

### Difficulty knobs

- Number of simultaneous contracts.
- Cargo incompatibility.
- Route hazard density.
- Police suspicion.
- Robber interest.
- Truck condition.
- Weather severity.
- Available strap anchors.
- Time-of-day visibility.
- Number of required truck transfers.
- Customer patience.
- Optional high-value objectives.

Difficulty should scale down for solo players by reducing simultaneous tasks and object mass requirements—not merely weakening enemies.

## Rhythm, quiet and beautiful roads

Constant chaos becomes tiring and eventually stops being funny. A workday can move through:

1. Depot and loading chaos.
2. Peaceful departure.
3. Subtle warning signs.
4. A major incident.
5. Protected recovery driving.
6. Final approach and escalation.
7. A physical drop-off payoff.

After a major incident, the game should deliberately suppress new attacks for a while. Let players repair damage, re-stack cargo, switch seats, listen to the radio and talk while the truck passes somewhere genuinely beautiful.

Calm-route possibilities:

- A sunset coastal highway with whales offshore.
- A quiet alpine pass after a snowstorm.
- Forest roads lit by fireflies and the truck headlights.
- A tiny sleeping village where using the horn would be socially disastrous.
- A long ferry crossing with calm water and distant islands.
- Open desert beneath an enormous night sky.
- Rain on the skylights while the road temporarily becomes easy.
- A scenic lookout where stopping is optional but tempting.
- Radio songs that players gradually associate with surviving terrible jobs.

## Keep-moving pressure

Use a distance buffer more often than a binary countdown. Speed and shortcuts build distance from the threat; slow driving, crashes and stopping consume it. Being caught should first change the situation rather than instantly end the run.

- Avalanche consumes the road behind the truck.
- Wildfire, floodwater or toxic fog advances across the map.
- Police pursuit gains vehicles and roadblocks over time.
- Robbers move from scouts to boarding parties to a full convoy.
- A gigantic creature follows the cargo’s scent.
- Darkness releases increasingly dangerous wildlife.
- The destination ferry, train, ship or aircraft is preparing to leave.
- A bank dye bomb or unstable cargo worsens when the truck stops.
- A bridge network collapses one segment at a time behind the crew.

Keep-moving missions should be a distinct contract family, not the rule for every journey. Pressure can also come from customer patience, cargo state, suspicion and optional reward multipliers.

### The old man collection

An extremely slow, fragile old man comes out to collect the package personally. He refuses help, stops to tell stories, checks the label several times and inches toward the truck with a tiny trolley. Meanwhile, increasingly desperate robbers attack from every side.

The crew must defend him and the cargo without knocking him over, driving away or frightening him into starting the walk again. He remains completely calm, occasionally blocks a shot with his trolley and insists on finishing a lengthy signature after reaching safety.

Variants:

- He forgets his glasses halfway through the signature.
- He selected the slowest possible payment method.
- The “small parcel” requires him to make several trips.
- Robbers politely wait while he crosses their line of fire, then resume fighting.
- He turns out to be terrifyingly capable only after the players have nearly lost.
- The actual customer is his even slower older brother.

Use original parody archetypes and designs, not recognisable copyrighted heroes or monsters. The central joke is that the players are not saving the city; they still need to get a refrigerator delivered before 5pm.

World-event escalation levels:

1. **Spectacle:** animation, dialogue and sound only.
2. **Collateral:** debris, traffic, route closures or frightened NPCs reach the road.
3. **Convergence:** the event unexpectedly becomes part of the delivery finale.

Most events should stay at level one. A route forecast can hint at the category without promising the outcome: `Significant vigilante activity expected downtown. Delivery delays not covered by insurance.`

## Action acknowledgement

Every story-worthy player action should leave a physical mark, change a state, provoke a reaction or create a future consequence. Record the causal shape as `Actor + Action + Target + Method + Result + Witnesses`.

Acknowledgement occurs through:

1. Immediate animation, sound, physical marks and NPC response.
2. Changed behaviour and consequences during the current workday.
3. Specific inspection, dialogue, payment and alternatives at drop-off.
4. Later radio reports, complaint letters, follow-up contracts, depot trophies and recurring nicknames.

### If the dinosaur dies

Do not reduce the outcome to `cargo damaged`. Remember whether it hatched, whom it imprinted on, what it ate, who killed it, how it died, its final condition and who witnessed the event.

- The scientist accepts it as a specimen for reduced payment.
- A museum offers an immediate body-transport contract.
- A restaurant secretly offers more than the original customer.
- The crew disguises it as sleeping.
- A captured robber is placed inside a dinosaur costume as a substitute.
- Insurance requests a death certificate and disputes its date of birth.
- The customer hires the crew to retrieve a cloning machine.
- Its parent follows the truck during a later workday.
- The player it imprinted on gains a `Dinosaur’s Mother` reputation trait.
- The depot installs an awkward memorial.
- Radio news reports the incident using completely incorrect details.
- A frozen, exploded, starved, crushed or deliberately killed dinosaur produces a different reaction.

Generic cargo can combine tagged states with reusable reaction templates. Signature cargo and recurring customers receive authored overrides so the most memorable outcomes feel genuinely recognised.

### Gradual bunkerisation

The depot begins as a shabby warehouse and comforting garage. Persistent upgrades make it look increasingly like a bunker while ordinary customers continue using the front parcel counter.

- Reinforced shutters that close painfully slowly.
- Roof spotlights operated by hand.
- Improvised barricades built from rejected cargo.
- Secure vaults and less-secure false walls.
- A defensive forklift with excellent armour and no brakes.
- Rooftop harpoon officially registered as loading equipment.
- A suspiciously military parcel scanner.
- An escape truck that is always parked behind another truck.
- Sandbags embroidered with the company logo.
- A panic room containing only the depot cat.

### Rare depot-defence events

Defence should be an announced chapter event, not a routine wave after every shift. The goal is usually to protect trucks, tools, customers and stored cargo rather than eliminate every attacker.

- Robbers raid the stored high-value contracts.
- Repo agents arrive to seize the fleet.
- A dinosaur escapes inside the warehouse.
- A rival company launches a literal hostile acquisition.
- Police arrive while criminals attack from the opposite side.
- The crew defends the depot during a company barbecue.
- A giant battle outside sends debris through the roof.
- An insurance inspector documents damage while the attack continues.
- Sentient cargo opens the shutters for the attackers.
- Players must evacuate the depot into several trucks while defending the loading bay.
- The depot cat repeatedly walks through the security sensors.

Failure damages equipment, loses stored cargo or creates a retrieval mission rather than deleting progression. Give advance warning so players can choose defensive preparation, move valuable stock or schedule the defence before starting another exhausting run.

## Persistent company progression

- Unlock regions and ferry routes.
- Acquire new truck classes.
- Earn licences for hazardous, magical and theoretically alive cargo.

## Capability-changing unlocks

These are post-V1 progression ideas, not launch requirements. Prefer unlocks that add a physical verb, coordination problem, recovery option or dangerous trade-off over flat percentage improvements.

### Convoys and vehicle modules

- Multi-truck convoy contracts split the crew between two vehicles.
- Players transfer cargo or jump between trucks while both are moving.
- One truck can tow, push or recover the other.
- Split-route contracts force the crews to separate and reunite later.
- Trailers can detach accidentally and be recovered by another truck.
- Modular trailer bodies include freezer, flatbed, tanker, livestock cage, mobile bank vault, aquarium and suspicious laboratory.

### Universal tools

- Recovery winch for cargo, teammates, robbers and entire vehicles.
- Portable ramps that create shortcuts or launch incoming objects.
- Foam cannon that cushions cargo, blocks doors and makes roads slippery.
- Inflatable crash mats that rescue falling players and bounce explosives.
- Grappling hook for climbing, retrieval and unintended truck-to-truck attachment.
- Cargo drone that can lift one item and is easily overloaded.
- Emergency parachutes for packages, players or the wrong truck component.
- Telescopic pole for distant buttons, loose cargo and irritating the driver.
- Magnetic boots or gloves that solve one problem and create several others.

### Powerful upgrades with drawbacks

- Armoured doors are secure but painfully slow.
- Industrial winches are strong enough to tear fragile cargo apart.
- Improved suspension reduces damage but produces enormous rebounds.
- Larger windows improve awareness but break easily.
- Roof racks add capacity and attract roof-climbing robbers.
- Automatic straps secure cargo and occasionally choose the wrong object.
- Better engines increase speed, fuel use, heat and braking distance.
- False walls hide illegal cargo while shrinking usable interior space.

### Company permissions and services

- Licences for biological, explosive, supernatural, government, celebrity and interdimensional deliveries.
- Route privileges such as ferry membership, airport access, diplomatic plates, an illegal tunnel key and permission to use one inexplicable private bridge.
- Emergency cargo recall, vehicle towing and single-package airlift services that occur physically and can go wrong.
- Insurance policies with excellent payouts and absurd conditions, such as explosions being covered only while stationary.
- Reputation paths for respectable courier work, criminal logistics, scientific transport and paranormal specialists; paths overlap rather than permanently locking content.

### Mid-run opportunities

- Radio contracts offer unexpected pickups while the truck is already moving.
- Temporary passengers include a mechanic, armed guard, inspector, hitchhiker, customer, rival courier or suspiciously qualified child.
- Roadside vendors sell one-run equipment with poorly explained side effects.
- A stranded truck offers cargo, passengers or a temporary second vehicle.
- Customers can increase the reward by changing requirements after pickup.

### Company memory and personality

- Trucks retain names, dents, repair patches, stickers and trophies across workdays.
- The company handbook records discovered cargo reactions, customer habits and handwritten crew warnings.
- Collectible radio tapes add songs, advertisements, traffic reports and concerning CEO announcements.
- Depot facilities include a test track, cargo cannon, vehicle lift, firing range, aquarium, defensive forklift and employee lounge.
- Physical souvenirs from completed disasters accumulate around the garage.
- Dashboard toys and mascots react to impacts, weather and supernatural cargo.

The most transformative late-game unlock is convoy work. It changes team structure while reusing driving, climbing, cargo, route and recovery systems already central to the game.

## Candidate escalation chains

### The egg chain

1. Heater keeps dinosaur egg warm.
2. Hard corner knocks heater toward explosives.
3. Crew opens window to cool explosives.
4. Vampire wakes in sunlight.
5. Vampire opens egg crate for shade.
6. Dinosaur hatches and eats the route map.

### The ferry chain

1. Truck is loaded without handbrake.
2. Rough wave moves it into another vehicle.
3. Customs officer begins an inspection.
4. Giant magnet pulls confiscated cargo back through both trucks.
5. Ferry loses power.
6. Crew must tow the ferry with the damaged truck.

### The bank chain

1. Crew accepts a high-value cash contract.
2. Rival couriers leak the route.
3. Robbers board through the roof.
4. Driver takes a low-clearance shortcut.
5. Roof and robbers are removed together.
6. Loose cash escapes into a street parade.

### The truck-swap chain

1. Refrigeration fails.
2. Crew diverts to a ferry terminal for a replacement truck.
3. Only a flatbed is available.
4. Storm begins during physical transfer.
5. Cargo must be strapped while the ferry departs.
6. The flatbed is faster, unlocking a dangerous bonus contract.

## Things to protect during production

- The player can understand why an object moved.
- Grabbing remains predictable even when arms look ridiculous.
- Truck interiors remain visually readable for six players.
- Windows keep rear players connected to road events.
- Chaos occurs in waves rather than continuously.
- Quiet loading, recovery and beautiful driving remain meaningful.
- Major incidents are followed by protected breathing room.
- The depot remains a comforting home even as it becomes an absurd bunker.
- Most background battles remain unrelated spectacle rather than compulsory quests.
- Story-worthy actions receive immediate, arrival and persistent acknowledgement.
- Non-driver activities transform the situation rather than becoming repetitive maintenance chores.
- Arrival is physical gameplay rather than an automatic completion zone.
- Truck changes feel like special chapters, not routine menus.
- Story never prevents friends from quickly starting a workday.
- Random events remix authored spaces rather than generating bland roads.
- Every new mechanic creates social decisions or visible physical comedy.
